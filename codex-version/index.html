01 / Check your initial state

Use the initial condition to find the coefficients.

For a time-independent Hamiltonian, we already know the general solution:

ψ(x,t) = Σₙ cₙ 𝜑ₙ(x)e−iEₙt/ℏ

The energy eigenfunctions 𝜑ₙ(x) and energies Eₙ are known. Our task is to determine the cₙ from the given initial wave function, just as we determined coefficients in the energy basis for quantum states.

The particle is confined to 0 < x < L. At t = 0, its state is

ψ(x,0) = A sin(πx/L)[1 + cos(πx/L)],
A > 0, chosen so ∫0L|ψ|2dx=1.

At t = 0, every exponential is 1, so ψ(x,0) = Σₙ cₙ𝜑ₙ(x). Use the overlap equation at right to find the coefficients. Enter c₁ and c₂ from your derivation.

Why do the other coefficients vanish?

Use sin θ cos θ = ½ sin 2θ to express the initial wave function in terms of 𝜑₁ and 𝜑₂. Orthonormality then makes its overlap with every 𝜑ₙ for n ≥ 3 zero.

Use decimals or expressions such as 1/sqrt(3). Inputs are evaluated locally; your answers are not uploaded.

Connect the two representations

𝜑ₙ(x) = ⟨x|Eₙ⟩
ψ(x,0) = ⟨x|ψ(0)⟩
|ψ(0)⟩ = Σₙ cₙ|Eₙ⟩
cₙ = ⟨Eₙ|ψ(0)⟩
= ∫0L𝜑n*(x)ψ(x,0)dx

The same coefficients describe the state in the energy basis and its wave function in position space. The probability of measuring Eₙ is |cₙ|².

Your basis

𝜑ₙ(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)
Eₙ = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²)

Each 𝜑ₙ is normalized. Different energy eigenstates are orthogonal.

What are we checking?

Your coefficients must reconstruct the given initial shape, including its relative sign, and give total probability one.

Keep your handwritten work nearby. A useful check tells you where to look again; it does not replace the derivation.

Put your coefficients into the general solution.

Use this form for your prediction. At t = 0 the two sine terms add with positive coefficients. As time passes, their different phase factors change how they interfere in |ψ(x,t)|².

Show the interference expression

For a general superposition: |ψ|² = Σₙ |cₙ|²𝜑ₙ² + 2 Σₙ<ₘ Re[cₙ* cₘ exp(−i(Eₘ−Eₙ)t/ℏ)] 𝜑ₙ𝜑ₘ. For the two-state controls, this reduces to:

The eigenfunctions are real. The first two terms are fixed; the relative phase controls the cross term. The cross term integrates to zero by orthogonality.

What the model assumes

A nonrelativistic particle, perfectly rigid infinite walls, and unitary time evolution. No measurements or interactions are applied during the animation.

The animation is a probability distribution—not a particle trajectory.

How this is calculated

The app evaluates the exact analytic sum of the selected two energy eigenstates, each multiplied by exp(−iEₙt/ℏ). It samples that expression for plotting; it does not numerically integrate the Schrödinger equation. The class coefficients follow from sin θ cos θ = ½ sin 2θ and normalization. No eigenstate truncation is needed for these initial states.

An investigation beyond two states

Build a packet. Predict its motion. Meet the wall.

Use the same energy eigenstates and time-evolution rule as before. This time, a localized initial state needs many terms.

How many energy levels do you need?

Your class state needed exactly two. Here the target is a smooth bump near x/L = 0.30, with zero amplitude at both walls.

ψ(x,0) = A sin(πx/L) exp[−(x−x₀)²/(4σ²)],
x₀ = 0.30L,   ∫0L|ψ(x,0)|2dx=1.

The sine factor enforces the boundary conditions. A normalizes the target. The width parameter σ controls localization; it is not exactly the standard deviation after multiplying by the sine.

How do we find the coefficients?
cₙ = ⟨Eₙ|ψ(0)⟩ = ∫0L𝜑n*(x)ψ(x,0)dx
ψN(x,0) = Σₙ₌₁ᴺ cₙ 𝜑ₙ(x)

This is the same overlap integral as in the two-state calculation. Here we evaluate it by composite Simpson quadrature. Increasing N reduces basis truncation error; increasing the quadrature resolution addresses a different numerical error.

Why does this packet move to the right?

The initial density tells us where the particle is likely to be found. To predict which way its average position will initially move, we also need its average momentum.

The real packet we just constructed has ⟨p̂⟩ = 0 initially. Now prepare a packet with the same initial density and positive average momentum. Which way should its average position begin to move?

d⟨x̂⟩/dt = ⟨p̂⟩/m

Positive momentum means an initially increasing ⟨x̂⟩, toward the right. Zero average momentum means zero initial slope of ⟨x̂⟩; the density can still spread.

How do we give the packet positive average momentum?

The factor eik₀x is the spatial part of a momentum eigenfunction with p₀ = ℏk₀. Multiplying our localized wave function by this factor gives it average momentum p₀ while keeping the same initial density. Here k₀ > 0, so p₀ > 0.

ψmoving(x,0) = ψ(x,0)eik₀x,   k₀ = 18π/L
|ψmoving(x,0)|² = |ψ(x,0)|²

The localized packet contains a range of momenta. Its average momentum sets the initial direction of motion; its different energy components also allow it to spread.

Verify the average momentum with the momentum operator
p̂ = −iℏ ∂/∂x
p̂[ψ(x,0)eik₀x] = eik₀x[ℏk₀ψ(x,0) − iℏ ∂ψ(x,0)/∂x]
⟨p̂⟩moving = ⟨p̂⟩real + ℏk₀ = ℏk₀

For the normalized real initial packet, integration of ψ ∂ψ/∂x gives zero because ψ vanishes at both walls. Its initial momentum expectation value is therefore zero. Multiplying by eik₀x gives a positive initial momentum expectation value. The derivative of the envelope is also present, so the packet has a range of momenta. Both packets can spread.

A constant overall phase leaves the physical state unchanged. This phase varies with position and changes the state. Recompute cₙ for each initial wave function, then apply the same energy-dependent time factors.

What is exact, and what is numerical?

The basis eigenfunctions and energies are analytic. Coefficients are computed numerically using 2,048 Simpson intervals. The animation keeps n = 1…64, normalizes that projection, and applies exp(−iEₙt/ℏ) to each coefficient. It does not integrate the Schrödinger equation in time.

The 64-state solution approximates the specified target packet. See the reconstruction step to explore truncation.
