Why are c₃, c₄, … all zero?

With sin θ cos θ = ½ sin 2θ, ψ(x, 0) = A sin(πx/L) + (A/2) sin(2πx/L). That is already a sum of φ₁ and φ₂, and orthogonality makes every other overlap vanish.

At t = 0 the two terms add on the left half of the well and partly cancel on the right. Their phases then advance at different rates.

At T/4 the density is symmetric about L/2. Which term of |ψ|² vanishes there? At T/2 it is the mirror image of t = 0. Why? (How does φ₁φ₂ behave under x → L − x?)

Show the interference term
|ψ|² = c₁²φ₁² + c₂²φ₂² + 2c₁c₂ φ₁φ₂ cos[(E₂ − E₁)t/ħ]

Only the cross term depends on time, and it integrates to zero, so total probability stays 1. The density repeats after T = 2πħ/(E₂ − E₁) = (2π/3) ħ/E₁. ψ itself only returns after 3T, when both phases come back together.

Questions to try

• Two states: move |c₂| to 0 or 1. Re ψ keeps changing, so why does the density stop moving?

• Two states: change δ. What does the relative phase change, and what does it leave alone?

• Ax(L − x) uses only odd n. Find the first time its density returns to its starting shape. Why is that so much sooner than 2πħ/E₁?

• Any state: press “t = 2πħ/E₁”. Every state returns exactly. Why must that happen in this well?

What this page assumes and computes

A nonrelativistic particle, perfectly rigid walls and undisturbed time evolution. The density is a probability distribution, not a trajectory.

Every curve is an exact sum of energy eigenstates, each with its e^(−iEₙt/ħ). Nothing integrates the Schrödinger equation. For Ax(L − x) the page uses cₙ = 8√15/(nπ)³ for odd n, summed to n = 39.

Units: x in L, ψ in L−1/2, |ψ|² in 1/L, t in ħ/E₁. For an electron in a 1 nm well, E₁ = 0.376 eV and ħ/E₁ = 1.75 fs.
